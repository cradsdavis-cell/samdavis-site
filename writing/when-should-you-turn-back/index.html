When Should You Turn Back?

On wisdom, intuition, and whether a machine can know when to turn back.

Sam Davis · Sydney · October 2026

The hail

A few years ago I was in the Swiss Alps with my climbing partner, going to do a big mountaineering day out on the Joderhorn. We’d hiked in for about two or three hours and were nearly at the bottom of the route, about to start. Clouds came overhead, and it started hailing, only lightly.

I turned to my climbing partner and said, “I think we should turn back.”

We did. I just listened to my intuition. It felt like the right thing to do, even though at the time it wasn’t glaringly obvious that it was the call we should make.

As we were walking back, thunder and lightning came over the mountain we’d been about to climb. We would have been caught in it.

Gathering everything

There’s an idea that the wise thing is to question everything and gather all of the information before making a decision. I’ve realised that isn’t actually wisdom.

Generally, gathering evidence is a good thing, and hyperintelligent AI will do a lot of it. But the degree of agency matters too. If you look at people in the real world, people with high agency tend to do very well. People who are overly analytical and fall into analysis paralysis are not effective. Sometimes people with high agency who aren’t self-aware or analytical enough are reckless and end up making poor choices.

So there’s a balance between agency and evidence. I don’t think that’s the discussion about wisdom. Wisdom is a separate thing, and it comes from a degree of intuition.

Stopping rules

Think of the wisest person you know, and take things away from them one at a time. Take away their intuition and they’d probably become more analytical, maybe paralysed. Take away their principles and they’d become indecisive. Take away their empathy and they’d become cold, callous and manipulative in just getting what they want.

Two of those end in the same place. Intuition and principles do the same job: they tell you when you know enough to act. The overthinker has plenty of evidence and no way of knowing when to stop.

If the sky had cleared ten minutes after we turned round, turning back would still have been the right call. On a mountain the two mistakes aren’t equal. A wrong “go” can kill you. A wrong “turn back” costs you a day. Wise caution is in proportion to what you could lose. The overthinker is just as cautious when nothing much is at stake.

You can’t judge an intuition on one call, either. If I turned back every time a cloud went over, my intuition would need some fine-tuning.

Getting in the water

Some people are born with a bit of intuition. Some people are just quite sensitive. But mostly I think you build it. Out of your comfort zone, a lot. In places where you don’t fully understand what’s going on. Around lots of different sorts of people from all sorts of cultures.

I wonder whether you can read your way to it at all. Intuition is an unspoken thing. You can’t fully describe it or put it into words. The philosopher Michael Polanyi put it as “we can know more than we can tell” (Polanyi, 1966). It’s like learning how to swim by watching a YouTube video about it. You have to actually go and get in the water.

AI is starting to get in the water. Agents now learn by trial and error, millions of times over. An AI trained that way would at least know how to swim. Whether it would have turned back below the Joderhorn is another question.

Preached and lived

Wisdom on its own is just a collection of facts that you can preach. A language model has read more of it than anyone alive, and can preach all of it.

Marcus Aurelius wrote Meditations as a private notebook, for nobody but himself (Kamtekar, 2025).

Wisdom with consciousness and awareness is the ability to always question your wisdom and always be mindful.

Awareness and care

A good con artist is extremely aware. He reads his mark perfectly and he’s completely present. Is he wise? No. He’s missing empathy. He has awareness, maybe, but not mindfulness, because he isn’t deeply understanding the position of his mark.

Research on psychopathy describes something similar: people who can read what others think and intend, but don’t respond to their distress the way most people do (Blair, 2005).

In the Buddha’s Eightfold Path it’s right mindfulness, one of eight factors alongside right intention (Bodhi, 1994).

One of the wisest characters I know is a cartoon: Uncle Iroh in Avatar: The Last Airbender (DiMartino and Konietzko, 2005 to 2008), who lets his nephew make his own mistakes without ever stopping caring about him.

Wisdom is really hard to define, and I think that’s the crux.

Can a machine care?

AI is getting very good at the awareness part. I think care, at its core, needs feeling, at least for us to be able to trust it and for it not to be manipulative. Otherwise it does just feel like manipulation. And nobody can yet check whether an AI feels anything at all.

We can never fully trust AI, in the same way we can never fully trust anyone. But we build trust with people slowly, and we can build trust with AI slowly. The issue is superintelligence. Once AI can improve itself (Good, 1965), the development of trust will probably lag behind the development of the intelligence, because we won’t be able to keep up with it anymore.

Anthropic wrote this year: “An agent that stops at every possible question will give up most of the autonomy that makes it useful; one that always pushes through will risk misreading what the user really intended” (Anthropic, 2026).

Can a machine learn to make the call I made below the Joderhorn: turn back when it matters, and only then?
