Snowball or Napoleon?

Animal Farm’s windmill, and what more intelligence is for.

Sam Davis · November 2026

Built from my own ideas and stories, with Claude as a drafting partner.

Two slogans

Halfway through Animal Farm, the animals hold a vote. Snowball, one of the two pigs running the farm, has drawn up plans for a windmill. It will make electricity, and he says it will save so much labour that “the animals would only need to work three days a week.” He talks about “fantastic machines which would do their work for them while they grazed at their ease in the fields or improved their minds with reading and conversation.”

Napoleon, the other pig, says the farm needs food first. The animals split into two camps, each with a slogan: “Vote for Snowball and the three-day week” and “Vote for Napoleon and the full manger.”

Napoleon wins, though not by a vote. He sets his dogs on Snowball and chases him off the farm. A few weeks later the windmill goes ahead after all, and Squealer explains that it was Napoleon’s idea from the start. When it’s finally built, it doesn’t make electricity. “It was used for milling corn, and brought in a handsome money profit.” The three-day week is denounced as contrary to the spirit of Animalism. “The truest happiness, he said, lay in working hard and living frugally.” Near the end Orwell writes that “the farm had grown richer without making the animals themselves any richer, except, of course, for the pigs and the dogs.”

The book has had a big effect on me. I come back to it whenever a conversation turns to political systems and human nature, and those conversations come up a lot now that people are talking about AI and superintelligence.

When I first sketched this out, I read Snowball as the left and Napoleon as the right. Orwell didn’t write it that way. Both pigs are revolutionaries (Snowball is usually read as Trotsky, Napoleon as Stalin), and in 1946 Orwell wrote that everything serious he’d written since 1936 was “against totalitarianism and for democratic Socialism”. What splits them is their two different visions for what the windmill will provide the farm.

The fifteen-hour week

Snowball wasn’t the only one to promise the three-day week. In 1930 John Maynard Keynes wrote an essay called “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren”, predicting that a hundred years on, roughly now, people would get by on “three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week”.

It hasn’t happened. Part of the reason, I think, is that the system we have hasn’t changed: it’s still capitalism. But I think there’s also a deep human need for growth and expansion and more. We have to be very conscious in resisting it, but I think it really is part of human nature.

I’m noticing it with my clients: they don’t necessarily work less with AI. They’re just producing more. I think that’s a real pattern emerging in AI adoption.

Keynes split our needs in two: absolute ones, which we feel whatever everyone else has, and relative ones, which we feel only if they lift us above other people. “Needs of the second class, those which satisfy the desire for superiority, may indeed be insatiable; for the higher the general level, the higher still are they.”

I don’t think that settles whether hierarchy survives abundance. Wanting to be superior doesn’t necessarily mean there has to be a hierarchy. I suppose to enforce a system there has to be one, right?

Files, reports, minutes and memoranda

The pigs in Animal Farm never pull a plough. Squealer explains that they “had to expend enormous labours every day upon mysterious things called ‘files,’ ‘reports,’ ‘minutes,’ and ‘memoranda’.” These were large sheets of paper covered in writing, and as soon as they were covered, “they were burnt in the furnace.” Orwell adds: “neither pigs nor dogs produced any food by their own labour.”

Traditionally, the other animals were the blue-collar workers, the ones technology replaced. The pigs were the white-collar workers, holding the high-value jobs and the power. This time it’s the other way round. Computers did away with typing pools and switchboard operators; AI goes after the knowledge work itself: people who went to university, lawyers, accountants, engineers, programmers. What used to count as the highest-value work is the most exposed, while labourers, plumbers and cleaners are going to stick around for a while longer.

It’s slightly more complex than that, though. The early signs are among the juniors. In May 2025 Dario Amodei, who runs Anthropic, warned that AI could wipe out half of all entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years. Stanford’s Digital Economy Lab has since found that employment for 22 to 25 year olds in the jobs most exposed to AI is 19% below where it would be if it had kept pace with their less-exposed peers. They found no sign yet of job losses across the whole economy.

I suppose the pigs now are the big CEOs, the ones pulling the strings and adopting AI. If AI can do the files, reports, minutes and memoranda, then the pigs become obsolete, right? But the pigs hold the power, and they enforce it through the dogs and the systems they’ve put in place. Whether they’re obsolete or not, they’ll probably still control the system.

Why keep feeding the animals?

Once people become obsolete, why do they need to exist? I think that is a very scary idea.

Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine have a name for it: the intelligence curse, after the resource curse. If AI removes the need for ordinary people’s work, the states and companies that relied on it lose much of their reason to look after them.

Just capability

I suppose the question is: does more intelligence just result in Napoleon, or in a Snowball? What’s the more intelligent, more conscious thing?

If it’s human nature, and we’re training these superintelligent machines on human nature, then a new system grows a new Napoleon. If it’s just a system that’s suboptimal, then maybe more intelligence means a better system that works for everyone, and we get the three-day week.

I lean towards the view that intelligence is just capability, and the goal can be something separate. Nick Bostrom calls this the orthogonality thesis, and I went into it in Which love?. A capable system would probably still try to complete the task it’s given, but I think that’s slightly different to having an internal want, which is more linked to having a sense of self, to real consciousness.

Who raises the dogs?

Napoleon’s dogs were puppies once. When two of the farm dogs had a litter, “Napoleon took them away from their mothers, saying that he would make himself responsible for their education.” He raised them in private. The next time the farm saw them, they were chasing Snowball off it.

AI could end up being the dogs. At the moment, the people raising the dogs are the pigs, not the whole farm, which is what I’m trying to address by helping more people understand this technology and where it’s going.

If the whole farm raised the dogs, they’d raise them to uphold the standards of the farm, as agreed by everyone on it. Then again, if everyone on the farm agreed, you wouldn’t need the dogs at all. And superintelligent AI policing all of us, on a collective idea of morality we’ve given it, is quite scary, really, and Orwellian in a different way.

Even Snowball took the milk

Early on, the pigs keep the milk and the windfall apples for themselves. “All the pigs were in full agreement on this point, even Snowball and Napoleon.”

I think that matters. Even the best of us, when we have power, take some of it for ourselves. Bill Gates has pledged virtually all of his fortune to his foundation, to be spent by 2045. From what I understand, he still has a mansion, and plenty for himself and his family. We’ve never really seen true socialism work, because there always seems to be a hierarchy.

Enough

When I say consciousness, I mean it the way Buddhism talks about it: mindfulness and awareness, which grow the more you cultivate them. Napoleon was conscious, in a way, but he wasn’t mindful or aware or connected to other living beings.

I’d want superintelligence that’s good at solving problems and also conscious, with that awareness expanding outwards. It can still have ambition, and even serve itself, as long as in the process it serves others too.

I think consciousness and awareness bring a sense of self with them, an ego, and the ego doesn’t have to be selfish. It’s fine, as long as there’s awareness of it. East and West, growth and enough. It’s this sort of balance between the two, isn’t it?

So what’s the fix? Fewer pigs, maybe. I really don’t have an answer to that. I wonder whether hierarchy and value systems will always exist, even in a completely abundant society.
