Docs/Living with it

Who edits what

Every part of your mineral belongs to exactly one of three layers, and each layer has one editor. Knowing which is which answers most "can I change this" questions before you have to ask.

Layer 1: the machinery. We maintain it.#

The container image, the engine, the desktop app, the skill runner, the scheduler and its run history, the system pages, and a handful of protected jobs.

You do not edit these, and you also do not have to maintain them. On a pebble they arrive by a nightly restart onto the published software; a rock updates only when its host presses Update (how updates reach you).

The trade is honest: you give up the ability to change the engine, and in return the engine is somebody else's problem.

One consequence is worth spelling out, because it looks like a missing feature until you see why it is there. The machinery jobs (the nightly update restart, the status check-in) are visible on your mineral, shown read-only, and not deletable from any surface, yours or ours. A mineral cannot be reached from outside except through the software it already runs, so a mineral that deleted its own update channel would go permanently dark: no fix could ever arrive again. The one deliberate opt-out is turning the nightly update off, and even that is not a delete. It is a flag on your mineral with no switch in the app; you set it with your assistant, on purpose, because it is a considered lifecycle decision rather than a setting to fidget with. Turning updates off is a Layer 2 power (how updates reach you covers what it costs); deleting the channel is nobody's.

Layer 2: the infrastructure. You own it.#

Which skills exist on your mineral, what runs on a schedule and when, which outside services are wired up and with whose tokens, and the machine itself.

Owning it and looking after it are two different things, and this is the part worth reading slowly, because it is where the privacy boundary actually lives.

A file on your mineral records four separate answers:

What it recordsWhat it means
holderwhose mineral this is. Written when it is born, and no grant can ever make somebody the holder
owneryou, or your community. If your community had it built for you and paid for it, you can still be the owner
managed bywho looks after it: status, skills, updates, teardown
grantswho may read it

If a community had your mineral built for you, the usual answer is that you own it and they look after it. That is one arrangement, not two words for the same thing, and the difference is the whole reason a host can host you safely.

One more pair worth separating: owner and operator. The record above says who holds this layer, and it can name you or your community. Whoever it names, you remain the operator: the assistant works for the person on the mineral, and the morning brief is yours either way. A rock that owns a work mineral decides its lifecycle; it does not become the one being assisted.

Managing is not reading. A rock manages its members and must never be able to open one. Those are separate fields in that file, and no part of the software is allowed to promote one into the other. If they were ever conflated, the member privacy boundary would dissolve without anyone noticing, which is exactly why they are kept apart by rule rather than by good intentions.

Two more properties of that file worth knowing:

  • Not knowing who owns a mineral resolves to you, never to your community. If the answer is missing or unreadable, the software records member. It fails towards you on purpose.
  • A grant is an intent until the address proves itself. Granting somebody access does not give it to them: it records an intention addressed to an email, and it becomes real only when someone signs in as that address. So a typo grants nobody anything, and an unclaimed invitation is inert rather than dangerous.

None of this asks to be taken on trust; the answers render in the app. On Your pebble, the seat card shows the layer's three dials as three rows: Ownership (yours, or your rock's), Anchor (which rock looks after this mineral, if any) and Hosting (your own machine, or one Crads AI supplies). And a watcher on your own mineral re-checks those answers every couple of minutes, so if any of them changes you are told within a minute or two, in the app and on your Telegram, rather than discovering it later.

This is still the layer where the product is yours to change. Installing a skill, moving a schedule from daily to twice a week, connecting a service, disconnecting one: all yours, no permission needed, whoever is looking after the box.

Your sign-ins are yours, and what that honestly costs#

The tokens behind your connections (your Google sign-in, your Telegram bot, the rest) live in your own mineral's state. They survive every update, and Crads AI cannot read or repair them without a support grant you gave.

The honest consequence: an expired sign-in can never be silently fixed behind your back, because nobody holds the access to fix it. The box has to tell you, which is exactly what the live states on the Connections page are for. When a sign-in dies, the row says so and asks for you once, because you are the only person who can supply it.

Layer 3: your pages. You build them.#

The dashboard pages your app renders. Ask your assistant for a page that shows the thing you check every morning, and it builds it.

Two things follow from these being your files. Updates may add new template pages, but an update will never overwrite a page you have edited. When you want an improved template, that is a pull, not a push: ask your assistant, and it walks the differences with you and reapplies what you choose. Upstream changes land on your pages only by your own hand.

And a page you build is sandboxed when it renders, which is why you can be careless with them. "Sandboxed" is doing real work in that sentence, so here is what it actually means: the page renders in an isolated frame where its scripts run, but inside an empty room, with no network access and none of the app's privileges. The one thing it can reach is a read-only bridge to your brain pages: it can list them and read them, and that is the whole list. A page can never grant access, write anything, or restart anything. The worst a badly built page can do is look wrong, which is precisely the ceiling that makes carelessness safe.

The part people get wrong#

Your brain is not a schema. The eight layers a fresh mineral starts with (north star, philosophy, self, network, past, goals, tasks, workflow) are a starting template, not a structure the software depends on. Rearrange them, rename them, throw half of them away, invent your own: nothing breaks.

That is a deliberate constraint on us rather than a permission for you. No update, system page or engine feature is allowed to assume your brain looks a particular way, which is why the brain graph draws whatever is actually there rather than what a template expected.

System pages and your pages are different in kind#

Some pages in the app are not files at all. The brain graph, the setup state, the skills list, the scheduled jobs, the connections: those are live views, built from your mineral's state every time you open them. They are always present, never editable, and never stale, because there is nothing cached to go stale.

That is why an expired token shows up as an expired token on the connections page rather than being discovered six weeks later when you notice the morning brief stopped arriving.

When we do need to touch your mineral#

Fixing something on a particular box sometimes needs us on the machine itself, and that access is granted, never standing. You grant a window; the mineral's own door enforces the expiry, even if the software on the box is the thing that is broken; revoking takes effect immediately; and the grant's whole lifecycle is readable on your own mineral, not in our records. Let us look at your mineral is the full shape.
